# Patient Guide: Eating for a Healthy Heart

## Why Diet Matters for Heart Health

What you eat has a direct and well-documented impact on your risk of heart disease, influencing blood pressure, cholesterol levels, blood sugar, and body weight, all of which are major drivers of cardiovascular risk. Dietary change is not a replacement for prescribed medication when medication is needed, but for many patients it meaningfully reduces the amount of medication required or slows the progression of risk factors that would otherwise require treatment escalation over time.

## Fats: Choosing the Right Kind

Not all dietary fat affects the heart the same way. Saturated fats, found in fatty cuts of red meat, butter, and full-fat dairy, tend to raise LDL ("bad") cholesterol and should be limited. Trans fats, historically found in many processed and fried foods, are particularly harmful and raise LDL while also lowering HDL ("good") cholesterol; many countries have restricted their use in food manufacturing as a result. Unsaturated fats, found in olive oil, nuts, seeds, and fatty fish such as salmon and mackerel, have the opposite effect, helping to lower LDL cholesterol when used in place of saturated fat, and are generally encouraged as part of a heart-healthy eating pattern.

## Sodium and Blood Pressure

Excess dietary sodium contributes directly to elevated blood pressure in many individuals, and reducing intake is one of the most effective dietary changes for blood pressure control. Most dietary sodium comes not from the salt shaker but from processed and restaurant foods, including canned soups, deli meats, packaged snacks, and fast food. Reading nutrition labels and choosing lower-sodium versions of common products, cooking more meals at home, and flavoring food with herbs and spices rather than salt are practical strategies that can meaningfully reduce daily intake without requiring a completely restrictive diet.

## Fiber and Whole Foods

Diets rich in fiber from vegetables, fruits, legumes, and whole grains are consistently associated with lower cardiovascular risk, partly through effects on cholesterol absorption and partly through improved blood sugar control and satiety, which supports healthy weight management. Aim to fill roughly half your plate with vegetables and fruits at most meals, choose whole grain versions of bread, rice, and pasta over refined versions, and incorporate legumes such as beans, lentils, and chickpeas regularly as both a fiber source and a lean protein alternative to red meat.

## Protein Sources

Choosing fish, poultry, legumes, and plant-based proteins over red and processed meat is associated with better cardiovascular outcomes. Fatty fish in particular, due to omega-3 fatty acid content, is specifically recommended at least twice weekly by most cardiovascular dietary guidelines. Processed meats, including bacon, sausages, and deli meats, are consistently associated with increased cardiovascular risk independent of their fat content, likely related to sodium and preservative content, and should be limited to occasional consumption rather than a dietary staple.

## Practical Meal Planning

Rather than aiming for perfection, focus on gradual, sustainable changes: swap one processed snack per day for a piece of fruit or a handful of unsalted nuts, replace one red meat meal per week with fish or a legume-based dish, and cook at home more often so you control the amount of added salt and fat. Planning meals a few days in advance and keeping a stock of simple staples such as frozen vegetables, canned beans, and whole grains makes it easier to prepare heart-healthy meals even on busy days without relying on takeout or highly processed convenience foods.

## Alcohol and Heart Health

If you drink alcohol, moderation is key, generally defined as up to one drink per day for women and up to two for men, though current guidance increasingly emphasizes that less is better regardless of baseline heart health, and patients with certain conditions such as arrhythmias, elevated triglycerides, or a history of heart failure may be advised to avoid alcohol altogether. Discuss your individual situation with your healthcare provider rather than assuming general population guidance applies uniformly to your specific risk profile.
