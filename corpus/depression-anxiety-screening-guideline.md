# Clinical Guideline: Screening for Depression and Anxiety in Primary Care

## Rationale for Screening

Depression and anxiety disorders are among the most common conditions encountered in primary care, yet they frequently go unrecognized, particularly when patients present with physical symptoms such as fatigue, sleep disturbance, or unexplained pain rather than explicitly reporting mood symptoms. Routine screening in primary care settings increases detection rates significantly compared to relying on unprompted patient disclosure, and earlier identification is associated with better treatment response and reduced risk of complications such as substance misuse or self-harm.

## Screening Tools for Depression

The Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) is among the most widely used and validated tools for depression screening, consisting of nine items corresponding to DSM criteria for major depressive disorder, each scored from zero to three based on frequency over the preceding two weeks. Total scores are generally interpreted as: 0-4 minimal, 5-9 mild, 10-14 moderate, 15-19 moderately severe, and 20-27 severe depression. A shorter two-item version, the PHQ-2, is often used as an initial screen, with a positive result prompting completion of the full PHQ-9. Importantly, item nine of the PHQ-9 specifically screens for thoughts of self-harm or suicide and requires direct follow-up regardless of the total score if endorsed.

## Screening Tools for Anxiety

The Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) scale serves a similar function for anxiety, with seven items scored similarly from zero to three based on frequency over the preceding two weeks. Scores of 5-9 suggest mild anxiety, 10-14 moderate, and 15-21 severe anxiety, with a score of 10 or greater generally considered a reasonable threshold for further clinical evaluation. Both the PHQ-9 and GAD-7 are screening instruments rather than diagnostic tools; a positive screen should prompt a full clinical assessment rather than being treated as a diagnosis in itself.

## Interpreting Results and Next Steps

A positive screen warrants a structured clinical interview to confirm diagnosis, assess symptom duration and functional impact, evaluate for other psychiatric comorbidities including substance use disorders, and rule out medical conditions that can mimic mood symptoms, such as thyroid dysfunction, anemia, or certain medication side effects. Given the substantial overlap and frequent co-occurrence of depression and anxiety, screening for one should generally prompt consideration of screening for the other, since treatment planning often needs to address both simultaneously.

## Suicide Risk Assessment

Any endorsement of suicidal ideation, whether through screening tools or direct disclosure, requires immediate and structured follow-up assessment covering frequency and intensity of thoughts, presence of a specific plan, access to lethal means, and protective factors such as social support and future-oriented goals. Clinicians should ask directly and non-judgmentally about suicidal thoughts, since direct questioning does not increase risk and is essential for accurate risk stratification. Patients assessed as being at acute risk require urgent referral to emergency psychiatric services rather than routine outpatient follow-up.

## Treatment Considerations Following Diagnosis

For mild depression or anxiety, watchful waiting with follow-up, guided self-help resources, or brief psychological interventions may be appropriate as initial management. Moderate to severe presentations generally warrant either psychotherapy, such as cognitive behavioral therapy, pharmacotherapy with agents such as selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors, or a combination of both, with combination treatment generally showing superior outcomes for more severe presentations compared to either modality alone. Regular follow-up using the same screening tool at subsequent visits allows objective tracking of treatment response over time rather than relying solely on subjective clinical impression.

## Special Considerations

Screening thresholds and tool validity may differ across populations, including adolescents, older adults, postpartum women, and patients with chronic medical conditions, where symptom overlap with the underlying illness can complicate interpretation. Cultural factors can also influence symptom expression and willingness to disclose psychological distress, and clinicians should remain alert to somatic presentations of mood disorders that may be more common in certain cultural contexts than direct emotional disclosure.
