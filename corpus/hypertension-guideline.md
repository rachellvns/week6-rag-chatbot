# Clinical Guideline: Management of Hypertension in Adults

## Classification of Blood Pressure

Blood pressure categories are used to guide treatment intensity. Normal blood pressure is defined as systolic under 120 mmHg and diastolic under 80 mmHg. Elevated blood pressure falls between 120 and 129 systolic with diastolic under 80. Stage 1 hypertension is defined as 130 to 139 systolic or 80 to 89 diastolic, while Stage 2 hypertension is 140 or higher systolic or 90 or higher diastolic. A hypertensive crisis, requiring immediate attention, is generally defined as readings above 180 systolic or 120 diastolic, especially when accompanied by signs of organ damage such as chest pain, shortness of breath, or visual disturbance.

## Initial Assessment

Before initiating treatment, clinicians should confirm the diagnosis using an average of two or more readings on two or more separate occasions, ideally supplemented by home or ambulatory monitoring to rule out white-coat hypertension. Baseline assessment should include evaluation for secondary causes in patients with resistant or early-onset hypertension, screening for target organ damage through renal function tests, urinalysis, electrocardiography, and fundoscopic examination where indicated, and a thorough review of cardiovascular risk factors including lipid profile, smoking status, and diabetes history.

## Lifestyle Modification

Lifestyle intervention is recommended for all patients regardless of pharmacologic therapy status. The DASH dietary pattern, emphasizing fruits, vegetables, whole grains, and low-fat dairy while reducing saturated fat and sodium, has demonstrated meaningful reductions in blood pressure. Sodium restriction to under 1500 to 2300 mg daily, weight loss in patients with overweight or obesity, regular aerobic exercise of at least 150 minutes per week, moderation of alcohol intake, and smoking cessation each contribute independently to blood pressure reduction and should be reinforced at every visit.

## Pharmacologic Therapy

For patients requiring medication, first-line agents generally include thiazide diuretics, calcium channel blockers, and angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors or angiotensin receptor blockers. Selection depends on comorbidities: ACE inhibitors or ARBs are preferred in patients with diabetes or chronic kidney disease due to renoprotective effects, while calcium channel blockers are often favored in older adults or those of African descent, who may show reduced responsiveness to renin-angiotensin system blockade alone. Beta-blockers are generally reserved for patients with specific compelling indications such as heart failure or prior myocardial infarction rather than as routine first-line therapy.

## Treatment Targets and Titration

Most guidelines now recommend a target blood pressure below 130/80 mmHg for the general adult population, with individualized targets for frail or elderly patients where overly aggressive lowering may increase fall risk. When a single agent fails to achieve target after an adequate trial period, combination therapy is generally preferred over maximizing the dose of a single drug, both to improve efficacy and to reduce dose-dependent side effects. Regular follow-up, typically every four to eight weeks during titration, allows for dose adjustment and monitoring for adverse effects such as electrolyte disturbance or renal function changes.

## Special Populations

In pregnancy, ACE inhibitors and ARBs are contraindicated due to teratogenic risk, and agents such as labetalol, nifedipine, or methyldopa are preferred instead. In patients with resistant hypertension, defined as blood pressure above target despite three or more agents including a diuretic, evaluation for secondary causes such as primary aldosteronism, renal artery stenosis, or obstructive sleep apnea should be pursued before adding a fourth agent, most commonly a mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist such as spironolactone.
