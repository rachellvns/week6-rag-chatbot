# Chronic Kidney Disease: Staging and Overview

## Definition

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is defined as abnormalities of kidney structure or function present for more than three months, with implications for health. This is typically established through evidence of reduced glomerular filtration rate (GFR) below 60 mL/min/1.73m², markers of kidney damage such as albuminuria, or structural abnormalities seen on imaging, sustained over this three-month period to distinguish chronic disease from acute kidney injury, which can present with similarly reduced filtration but resolves over a shorter time course.

## Staging System

CKD is staged using a combination of GFR category and albuminuria category, reflecting the understanding that both filtration loss and protein leakage independently predict outcomes. GFR categories range from G1, representing normal or high GFR above 90, through G2 (60-89), G3a (45-59), G3b (30-44), G4 (15-29), to G5, representing kidney failure with GFR below 15 or the need for dialysis. Albuminuria categories range from A1 (normal to mildly increased) through A2 (moderately increased) to A3 (severely increased). Combining these two dimensions produces a risk grid that guides both monitoring frequency and treatment intensity, with patients in higher-risk combined categories requiring more frequent nephrology involvement.

## Common Causes

The two leading causes of CKD in most populations are diabetes mellitus and hypertension, together accounting for the majority of cases requiring dialysis or transplantation. Diabetic kidney disease develops through a combination of glomerular hyperfiltration, basement membrane thickening, and progressive fibrosis driven by chronic hyperglycemia. Hypertensive kidney disease results from long-term pressure-related damage to the renal vasculature. Other important causes include glomerulonephritis, polycystic kidney disease, recurrent urinary tract obstruction, and prolonged use of certain nephrotoxic medications, particularly nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs when used chronically or in the setting of dehydration.

## Clinical Manifestations

Early CKD is typically asymptomatic and detected only through laboratory screening, which is why at-risk populations such as those with diabetes or hypertension require routine testing rather than waiting for symptoms. As kidney function declines further, patients may develop fatigue, fluid retention with swelling in the legs, reduced appetite, nausea, and difficulty concentrating. In advanced disease, more severe manifestations including pericarditis, peripheral neuropathy, and significant electrolyte disturbances such as hyperkalemia can occur, along with anemia related to reduced erythropoietin production and mineral bone disease related to disrupted calcium and phosphate metabolism.

## Monitoring and Complications Management

Patients with CKD require regular monitoring of GFR, albuminuria, electrolytes, hemoglobin, and markers of mineral bone disease, with frequency increasing as disease stage advances. Anemia of chronic kidney disease is typically managed with iron supplementation and, in appropriate candidates, erythropoiesis-stimulating agents once reversible causes have been excluded. Mineral bone disease is managed through dietary phosphate restriction, phosphate binders, and vitamin D analogs as needed, guided by regular laboratory monitoring rather than fixed dosing schedules.

## Slowing Progression

Several interventions have been shown to slow CKD progression. Blood pressure control, generally targeting below 130/80 mmHg, and use of ACE inhibitors or ARBs in patients with albuminuria provide both cardiovascular and renal protective benefits through reduction of intraglomerular pressure. Newer agents, including SGLT2 inhibitors, have demonstrated significant renal protective effects independent of their glucose-lowering action and are now recommended across a broad range of CKD patients regardless of diabetes status. Dietary protein moderation, avoidance of nephrotoxic medications, and smoking cessation are additional modifiable factors that contribute meaningfully to slowing disease progression.

## Preparing for Kidney Failure

For patients progressing toward kidney failure, typically anticipated when GFR approaches 15-20, early and thorough discussion of renal replacement options, including hemodialysis, peritoneal dialysis, and preemptive transplantation, allows adequate time for vascular access planning or transplant evaluation. Conservative, non-dialytic management is also an appropriate and increasingly recognized option for select patients, particularly older adults with significant comorbidity, for whom dialysis may not meaningfully extend or improve quality of life.
