# Clinical Summary: Antimicrobial Resistance in Primary Care

## Scope of the Problem

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) refers to the ability of bacteria, viruses, fungi, and parasites to withstand the drugs designed to kill them, rendering standard treatments ineffective and increasing the risk of disease spread, severe illness, and death. Global surveillance data consistently show rising rates of resistance across common pathogens, and resistant infections are associated with longer hospital stays, higher treatment costs, and increased mortality compared to infections caused by susceptible organisms. Primary care prescribing accounts for the majority of overall antibiotic use in most health systems, making outpatient prescribing behavior a central target for stewardship efforts.

## Mechanisms of Resistance

Bacteria develop resistance through several mechanisms, including enzymatic degradation of the antibiotic, such as beta-lactamase production against penicillins, alteration of the drug's target site so the antibiotic can no longer bind effectively, efflux pumps that actively expel the drug from the bacterial cell, and reduced membrane permeability that limits drug entry. Resistance genes can spread not only through vertical transmission during bacterial reproduction but also horizontally between different bacterial species via plasmids, a major driver of the rapid spread of multidrug-resistant organisms across unrelated bacterial populations.

## Drivers of Resistance

Inappropriate antibiotic use is the single most significant modifiable driver of resistance. This includes prescribing antibiotics for viral infections such as most upper respiratory tract infections, where they provide no benefit, using broad-spectrum agents when a narrow-spectrum drug would suffice, incorrect dosing or duration, and patient non-adherence to prescribed courses. Agricultural use of antibiotics in livestock, often for growth promotion rather than treatment of illness, is another significant contributor to the overall resistance burden, and international travel facilitates the global spread of resistant strains across borders.

## High-Priority Resistant Organisms

Certain pathogens are considered particularly concerning due to the severity of infections they cause and the limited remaining treatment options. Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) causes skin, soft tissue, and bloodstream infections resistant to standard beta-lactam antibiotics. Carbapenem-resistant Enterobacteriaceae (CRE) are resistant to a class of antibiotics often considered a last resort for gram-negative infections, and infections with these organisms carry mortality rates substantially higher than susceptible counterparts. Multidrug-resistant tuberculosis and drug-resistant Neisseria gonorrhoeae represent additional organisms of significant public health concern, with treatment options narrowing in some regions.

## Antimicrobial Stewardship Principles

Stewardship programs aim to optimize antibiotic use to improve patient outcomes while minimizing the development of further resistance. Core principles include prescribing antibiotics only when there is clear evidence of bacterial infection, selecting the narrowest effective spectrum agent, using the shortest effective duration supported by evidence rather than defaulting to traditional longer courses, and de-escalating therapy once culture results are available. Delayed prescribing strategies, where a prescription is provided but the patient is advised to fill it only if symptoms worsen or fail to improve within a specified period, have been shown to reduce unnecessary antibiotic consumption without compromising outcomes in appropriate clinical scenarios such as uncomplicated ear or sinus infections.

## Diagnostic Support

Point-of-care testing, including rapid strep tests, urine dipstick analysis, and C-reactive protein testing, can help clinicians distinguish bacterial from viral or self-limiting illness and reduce unnecessary prescribing. Clinical prediction rules, such as the Centor criteria for streptococcal pharyngitis, provide structured frameworks to guide testing and treatment decisions and have been shown to reduce inappropriate antibiotic use when consistently applied in practice.

## Patient Communication

Effective communication with patients is a critical but often underemphasized component of stewardship. Explaining why an antibiotic is not indicated, providing clear guidance on symptomatic management, and offering a specific timeline for expected symptom resolution along with clear return precautions can reduce patient dissatisfaction and repeat visits even when no antibiotic is prescribed, addressing one of the most commonly cited reasons clinicians report for prescribing unnecessarily.
