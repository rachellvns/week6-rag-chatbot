# Patient Guide: Recovering After Total Knee Replacement

## Before You Go Home

After your total knee replacement surgery, the hospital care team will make sure you can safely get in and out of bed, walk a short distance with a walker or crutches, and manage stairs if you have them at home, before you are discharged. Most patients stay in hospital for one to three days, though some centers now discharge suitable patients on the same day as surgery. You will be given a prescription for pain medication and, in many cases, a blood thinner to reduce the risk of blood clots, which you should take exactly as directed even if you are feeling well.

## Managing Pain and Swelling

Pain and swelling around the knee are expected for several weeks after surgery and generally improve gradually rather than all at once. Applying ice to the knee for 15 to 20 minutes several times a day, especially after exercise sessions, can help reduce both pain and swelling. Keeping the leg elevated above the level of your heart when resting, rather than letting it hang down, also helps limit fluid accumulation. Take pain medication on the schedule recommended by your surgical team rather than waiting until pain becomes severe, since it is easier to keep pain controlled than to bring it back down once it has escalated.

## Wound Care

Keep the surgical incision clean and dry according to the instructions given at discharge. Some drainage in the first few days is normal, but you should contact your surgical team promptly if you notice increasing redness spreading away from the incision, pus-like discharge, a foul odor, or if the wound becomes warm and increasingly painful rather than gradually improving, as these can be signs of infection. Do not soak the knee in a bath or swimming pool until your surgeon confirms the incision has fully healed, typically around two to four weeks.

## Physical Therapy and Movement

Early movement is one of the most important factors in a successful recovery. Physical therapy typically begins the day of or the day after surgery and continues for several weeks to months afterward, either in an outpatient clinic or through a home health program. Exercises focus on regaining range of motion, particularly knee bending and straightening, and rebuilding strength in the muscles surrounding the joint. Many patients find the first two to three weeks the most physically and emotionally challenging, but consistent, gentle progress with prescribed exercises is strongly associated with better long-term function, so it is important not to skip sessions even when motivation is low.

## Using Walking Aids

You will likely use a walker or crutches for the first few weeks, gradually progressing to a cane, and eventually walking unaided as strength and confidence improve. Follow your physical therapist's guidance on when it is safe to reduce your reliance on these aids rather than discarding them early, since doing too much too soon increases fall risk and can strain the healing joint.

## Warning Signs to Watch For

Contact your surgical team or seek urgent care if you experience sudden and significant swelling in the calf accompanied by pain or warmth, which can indicate a blood clot, chest pain or sudden shortness of breath, a fever above 38.5 degrees Celsius, or a sudden increase in knee pain accompanied by instability or the sensation that the joint is giving way. These symptoms should never simply be monitored at home and warrant prompt medical evaluation.

## Returning to Normal Activities

Most patients can resume light daily activities such as cooking and light household tasks within a few weeks, driving once off strong opioid pain medication and with adequate reflexes and strength (usually around four to six weeks), and low-impact activities such as swimming or cycling around six to eight weeks. Full recovery, including maximal improvement in strength and range of motion, can take up to a year, and it is normal for some residual stiffness or mild swelling to persist for several months even in an otherwise successful surgery.
