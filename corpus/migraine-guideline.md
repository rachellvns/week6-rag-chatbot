# Clinical Guideline: Diagnosis and Management of Migraine

## Diagnostic Criteria

Migraine is diagnosed clinically based on characteristic headache features rather than imaging or laboratory testing in the majority of cases. Typical criteria require at least five attacks lasting four to seventy-two hours if untreated, with headache features including at least two of unilateral location, pulsating quality, moderate to severe intensity, and aggravation by routine physical activity, accompanied by at least one of nausea or vomiting, or sensitivity to both light and sound. A subset of patients experience aura, a reversible neurological phenomenon typically involving visual disturbances such as flashing lights or zigzag lines, occurring shortly before or during the headache phase.

## When to Investigate Further

While most migraine can be diagnosed clinically, certain features should prompt consideration of neuroimaging or further investigation to rule out secondary causes. These "red flag" features include a sudden thunderclap onset, headache that is progressively worsening over weeks, new headache onset after age fifty, headache associated with fever, neurological deficits, altered consciousness, or headache triggered specifically by exertion, coughing, or changes in posture. Any of these features warrants a lower threshold for imaging or specialist referral rather than assuming a primary headache disorder.

## Acute Treatment

For acute attacks, treatment should be tailored to attack severity and prior treatment response. Mild to moderate attacks may respond adequately to simple analgesics such as ibuprofen or aspirin, ideally taken early in the attack before pain becomes severe. Moderate to severe attacks, or those not responding to simple analgesics, are typically treated with triptans, a class of drugs that act on serotonin receptors to abort the migraine mechanism; these should also be taken as early as possible in the attack for maximum effectiveness. Antiemetics such as metoclopramide can be added both to manage nausea and because they have some independent benefit on headache itself. Overuse of acute medications, generally defined as use on ten or more days per month for triptans or fifteen or more days per month for simple analgesics, risks causing medication-overuse headache, a distinct condition that paradoxically worsens headache frequency.

## Preventive Treatment

Preventive therapy should be considered for patients experiencing frequent attacks, typically four or more headache days per month, attacks that significantly impair function despite acute treatment, or contraindications to acute medications. First-line preventive options include beta-blockers such as propranolol, certain anticonvulsants such as topiramate, and tricyclic antidepressants such as amitriptyline, with selection guided by comorbidities and side-effect profile. For example, propranolol may be favored in patients with comorbid hypertension, while amitriptyline might be preferred in patients with coexisting insomnia or tension-type headache features. Newer options, including monoclonal antibodies targeting the CGRP pathway, are reserved for patients who have failed multiple conventional preventives, given their higher cost, though they have demonstrated strong efficacy and a favorable side-effect profile in clinical trials.

## Lifestyle and Trigger Management

Patients should be encouraged to maintain regular sleep schedules, consistent meal timing, adequate hydration, and regular exercise, as irregularity in these areas is a commonly reported trigger. Keeping a headache diary can help identify individual triggers, which vary considerably between patients and may include specific foods, hormonal fluctuations, weather changes, or stress. However, clinicians should avoid encouraging excessive dietary restriction based on unconfirmed trigger suspicion, since overly restrictive elimination approaches are rarely evidence-based and can negatively affect quality of life without proven benefit.

## Special Considerations in Women

Migraine is significantly more common in women, and hormonal fluctuations across the menstrual cycle, pregnancy, and menopause substantially influence disease course. Menstrual migraine, occurring in relation to the drop in estrogen preceding menstruation, may benefit from short-term preventive strategies timed around the cycle. During pregnancy, many standard preventive medications are contraindicated, and management typically shifts toward non-pharmacological strategies and careful selection of acute treatments known to have better safety profiles in pregnancy.
