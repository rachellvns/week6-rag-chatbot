# Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus: Overview and Pathophysiology

## Definition and Epidemiology

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is a chronic metabolic disorder characterized by insulin resistance and a progressive decline in beta-cell function, resulting in persistent hyperglycemia. It accounts for roughly 90 to 95 percent of all diagnosed diabetes cases worldwide and its prevalence continues to rise in parallel with obesity rates, sedentary lifestyles, and aging populations. Unlike type 1 diabetes, which results from autoimmune destruction of insulin-producing cells, type 2 diabetes typically develops over years, often silently, with many patients remaining undiagnosed until complications appear or the condition is discovered incidentally during routine bloodwork.

## Pathophysiology

The underlying disease process involves two interconnected defects. First, peripheral tissues — primarily skeletal muscle, liver, and adipose tissue — become resistant to the action of insulin, meaning that glucose uptake and suppression of hepatic glucose production are impaired despite adequate or even elevated circulating insulin levels. Second, pancreatic beta cells, which initially compensate by secreting more insulin, gradually lose their capacity to keep pace with demand. Over time, this combination of resistance and secretory failure produces the sustained hyperglycemia that defines the disease. Chronic hyperglycemia itself is toxic to beta cells and worsens insulin resistance, creating a self-reinforcing cycle that explains why the disease tends to progress even with treatment if lifestyle and pharmacologic interventions are not adequately maintained.

## Risk Factors

Established risk factors include excess body weight, particularly visceral adiposity, physical inactivity, a family history of diabetes, advancing age, and certain ethnic backgrounds that carry higher genetic susceptibility. Gestational diabetes during pregnancy and polycystic ovary syndrome also elevate future risk. Additionally, conditions such as hypertension and dyslipidemia frequently cluster with type 2 diabetes as part of the broader metabolic syndrome, and their presence should prompt screening even in patients without classic symptoms.

## Clinical Presentation

Many patients are asymptomatic at diagnosis. When symptoms do occur, they typically include increased thirst, frequent urination, unexplained fatigue, blurred vision, and slow-healing wounds. Because onset is gradual, patients may attribute early symptoms to aging or stress rather than seeking medical attention. Diagnosis is most often established through fasting plasma glucose testing, an oral glucose tolerance test, or measurement of glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), with HbA1c of 6.5 percent or greater on two occasions generally considered diagnostic.

## Long-Term Complications

Left inadequately controlled, type 2 diabetes produces both microvascular and macrovascular complications. Microvascular disease affects the retina, kidneys, and peripheral nerves, leading to diabetic retinopathy, nephropathy, and neuropathy respectively. Macrovascular disease accelerates atherosclerosis, substantially increasing the risk of myocardial infarction, stroke, and peripheral arterial disease. These complications develop over years and are strongly correlated with the degree and duration of glycemic control, which is why early diagnosis and sustained management are emphasized so heavily in clinical guidelines.

## Screening Recommendations

Current guidelines generally recommend screening adults beginning at age 35, or earlier in those with additional risk factors such as obesity, a first-degree relative with diabetes, or a history of gestational diabetes. Screening intervals of every three years are typical for those with normal results, though more frequent testing is warranted for patients with prediabetes, defined as an HbA1c between 5.7 and 6.4 percent, since this group carries a substantially elevated risk of progression to overt diabetes within five to ten years without intervention.
